A brief introduction

A brief introduction

I qualified as a Chartered Accountant in 2006 and began my career in the accounts and insurance functions at Torrent Pharmaceuticals. Two years later I became a promoter director of JS Assignments, and since then my work has moved steadily from recording numbers to designing the systems that produce them, and from advising businesses to helping build them.

Today that work spans virtual CFO support, MIS and automation, infrastructure execution, engineering, new business ventures, consumer brands and education. Along the way I added qualifications in information systems audit, insurance and risk management, and an MBA in insurance and financial planning, because each new problem asked for a new way of looking at it.

What connects all of it is a simple belief: good financial information should lead to better decisions, and good systems should make people more accountable, not less human.

My story

I am a Chartered Accountant by training and a builder by temperament. For twenty years I have worked where finance meets operations: closing books, designing reports, setting up controls, structuring deals, and more and more often, helping to run the businesses themselves. This page is the longer version of how that happened.

I.Early foundations

I was a studious boy, and from early on I knew what I wanted to do with it. I wanted to bring my head for commerce and my creative streak to real businesses, and to contribute to them in a way that showed. I studied commerce at The Maharaja Sayajirao University of Baroda, completing my B.Com in 2004, and in 2006 I finished my M.Com and qualified as a Chartered Accountant.

Looking back, the role I grew into was always going to be the virtual CFO. It is the one position that lets me contribute to many businesses at once, combining the discipline of finance with the creativity of building something.

II.Becoming a Chartered Accountant

I qualified as a Chartered Accountant in 2006. The articleship years taught me what textbooks could not: that every ledger reflects a set of human decisions, and that most errors are process failures before they are accounting failures.

III.Lessons from the corporate floor

My first role was as Assistant Manager in the accounts and insurance functions at Torrent Pharmaceuticals, from 2006 to 2008. Working inside a large organisation showed me how scale depends on routine: closing processes that run on time, insurance cover that is reviewed before it is needed, and controls that people actually follow. It was also my first serious exposure to risk and insurance, a thread that has stayed with me ever since.

“Financial information is only as valuable as the decision it improves.”

IV.Into enterprise

In 2008 I became a promoter director of JS Assignments Private Limited. The company grew in two directions that look different but share a discipline. On one side is finance and management consulting: virtual CFO support, MIS, compliance management and process improvement for owner-managed businesses. On the other is execution: road works carried out as a subcontractor on infrastructure projects. Running both taught me that a site schedule and a month-end close fail for the same reasons, and succeed for the same reasons too. From 2008 to 2011 I also practised part-time as a Chartered Accountant through Satvik Shah & Co.

V.Teaching alongside working

From 2010 to 2020 I was Associate Vice President at ICA Edu Skills, and from 2009 to 2011 I taught Insurance and Risk Management and the Accounting of Insurance Companies at The Maharaja Sayajirao University of Baroda, and Basic and Advanced Accounting, Economics and Business subjects at ICA. Teaching forces clarity. If I cannot explain a GST credit rule or a cash-flow statement to a first-year student, I probably do not understand it as well as I think. That habit of explaining things simply now runs through my advisory work, and through ARTHA ASCENT, the foundation programme we have built at JS Assignments.

VI.From accounting to systems

My DISA qualification in information systems audit changed how I look at finance teams. I stopped asking only whether the numbers were right and started asking whether the process that produced them could be trusted without me in the room. Much of my recent work is exactly that: expense approval portals, billing systems, daily book-closure checklists, accountability trackers, and reports that tell an owner what to do next instead of what happened last quarter.

VII.Engineering, new ventures and consumer brands

Since 2018 I have been a director of A&W Engineering Works (India) Private Limited, which works in ultrasonic and medical-device engineering. In 2021 I joined the board of 7E's Industries Private Limited, a company built to take innovative business ideas forward across sectors, and in 2023 Artkart Lifestyle Private Limited, an art-and-craft business. I am also CFO of Hyderabad Strikers in the Tennis Premier League, and act as CFO for engineering, infrastructure and consumer businesses, and I am closely involved with Avyukt World, a Krishna-themed devotional and lifestyle brand that is as much a personal passion as a business.

VIII.Risk, insurance and the long view

Risk has been a constant. After DIRM, I went back to study fourteen years after qualifying as a CA, completing an MBA in Insurance and Financial Planning at Chitkara University in 2022. I also advise families and business owners on life insurance as an agent of Life Insurance Corporation of India. I see insurance as a risk decision before it is a product decision, and I try to keep the conversation in that order.

IX.People, trust and discipline

Across every role, the lesson that repeats is that systems do not run businesses; people do. The best system is one that makes good work visible and makes it easy for people to do the right thing. I value straight answers, written commitments and follow-through, and I try to offer the same.

X.The years ahead

I am now a founding member of XCo, The Execution Company, and I am spending more time writing, mentoring and turning what I have learned into repeatable systems that other businesses can use. I want to help more owner-managed businesses in India make decisions with the clarity that large companies take for granted.

Before the dashboard: three questions every MIS should answer

Most management reports are accurate and ignored. The fix is not more data. It is deciding, before anything is built, what the report is for.

By Satvik ShahPublished · 15 Nov 2026— min read

Contents

Every month, in businesses of every size, someone spends two days preparing a report that the owner reads for two minutes. The numbers are right. The formatting is careful. And nothing changes as a result.

When I review a company's MIS, I rarely find errors in the arithmetic. What I find is a report built to show that work was done, instead of a report built to help someone decide. The difference sounds small. In practice it decides whether finance is a cost centre or a steering wheel.

Before anyone opens a spreadsheet or buys dashboard software, I ask three questions. They take an hour to answer properly, and they save months of reporting that nobody uses.

1. What decision is this report for?

Every line in a management report should connect to a decision someone can actually take. Revenue by product line matters if someone can change the product mix. Debtor ageing matters if someone will call the customer on Monday. A number that connects to no decision is trivia, however accurate it is.

The test is simple. For each line, write the name of the decision next to it. If you cannot, remove the line or move it to a supporting schedule. Most reports shrink by half, and become far more useful.

A dashboard with forty numbers is a list. A dashboard with four numbers is a decision.

2. What would make us act differently?

A number on its own says very little. Gross margin of 31 per cent is neither good nor bad until you know what you expected and what you would do if it were 27. So for each important line, agree a trigger in advance: the level at which someone must look closer, and the level at which someone must act.

Triggers turn a report from a description into an alarm system. They also remove a great deal of argument, because the conversation moves from whether a number is worrying to what we agreed to do when it reached this level.

3. Who owns the number?

A figure that belongs to everyone belongs to no one. Each line in the report needs a named owner: the person who explains it, who answers for it in the review meeting, and who acts when it crosses its trigger. The owner is not always the person who prepares it. Accounts can compile the debtor ageing, but the sales head owns collections.

Ownership is where most reporting systems quietly fail. Without it, the review meeting becomes a reading session. With it, the meeting becomes a short list of commitments.

Putting it together: a one-page test

Here is the format I use to test an existing report. The rows are illustrative, not drawn from any client.

Report lineDecision it supportsLook closer atAct atOwner
Debtors over 90 daysHold supplies, escalate, or provideAbove 10% of debtorsAbove 15%Sales head
Gross margin by productRe-price or change the mix2 points below budget4 points belowBusiness head
Cash runwayDraw on limits, defer capexBelow 90 daysBelow 60 daysFinance head
Statutory dues pendingPay now or plan the cashAny item due in 7 daysAny item overdueAccounts lead

Illustrative thresholds. Every business should set its own.

If a line in your current report cannot fill all five columns, it is a candidate for removal. If a decision you care about has no line, it is a candidate for addition.

What changes when MIS works

The report gets shorter. The meeting gets shorter too, and ends with actions instead of observations. Preparation time drops because people stop polishing numbers nobody uses. And once the report is organised around decisions, it becomes much easier to automate, because you know exactly which numbers matter and when.

None of this needs expensive software. It needs an hour of honest conversation before the first cell is filled. Start with the three questions. The dashboard can come later.
