Most people meet insurance as a product: a brochure, an illustration of returns, a friendly agent with a deadline. So most insurance decisions start with the question "which policy is good?" That is the wrong first question.

The right first question is "what could happen to my family or my business that we could not absorb on our own?" Insurance is simply the decision to transfer that risk to someone else at a known price. Once the risk is clear, choosing the product is the easy part.

## Step 1: name the risks you cannot absorb

For most families, the risks that matter fall into a short list:

- **Loss of income** if the earning member dies or cannot work.
- **A large medical expense** that would eat into savings meant for something else.
- **Liabilities that outlive you,** such as a home loan or a business guarantee.
- **Damage to a major asset,** such as a house, a vehicle or business stock.

For a business owner, add the risks to the business itself: key-person dependence, property and stock, liability to customers, and continuity if a partner dies.

## Step 2: put a number on each

A rough number beats a perfect brochure. For income protection, a common starting point is the income your family would need each year, multiplied by the years until your youngest dependant is independent, plus outstanding loans, less the savings and investments already available. You will refine it, but the order of magnitude is what matters at this stage.

The number is usually larger than people expect, and that is the point. It tells you how much cover you need before anyone shows you a policy.

## Step 3: separate protection from saving

This is where most confusion lives. Pure protection, such as term life cover, buys a large amount of cover for a modest premium. Savings-linked policies combine protection with an investment element, which can suit some goals, but they usually provide far less cover for the same premium.

There is no universally right answer. The discipline is to decide first how much protection you need, make sure you have it, and only then decide how much of your saving you want to route through insurance and how much elsewhere.

## Step 4: review at life events, not at renewal reminders

Cover that was right five years ago may be wrong today. The natural review points are marriage, a child, a new home loan, a business expansion, a large inheritance, and every few years in any case. A ten-minute review at each of these moments prevents the most common failure in insurance: being covered for the life you used to have.

## The questions to ask any adviser

1. What risk does this policy cover, and what does it not cover?
2. How much cover does it give for each rupee of premium?
3. What happens if I stop paying in year three?
4. How and how quickly are claims settled?

A good adviser answers these plainly. If the conversation keeps returning to returns, it is a product conversation, not a risk conversation.
