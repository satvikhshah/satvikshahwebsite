Owners usually call a virtual CFO for one of three reasons: the numbers arrive too late to be useful, the bank or an investor is asking questions nobody can answer quickly, or the business has grown faster than its systems. In each case the owner wants the same thing: to stop guessing.

I have worked as a virtual CFO since 2011, for businesses in sports, engineering, infrastructure, consumer goods and technology. The engagements that succeed look remarkably similar in their first three months. Here is what you should expect to see, and what you should ask about if you do not.

## Days 1 to 30: see the business as it really is

The first month is diagnosis, not reporting. A good virtual CFO will want to understand how money actually moves through the business: how orders become invoices, invoices become receivables, and receivables become cash. They will sit with the accounts team, look at the books, the bank statements and the statutory filings, and ask a lot of uncomfortable questions.

By the end of the month you should have:

- **A clean opening position.** Bank balances reconciled, debtors and creditors confirmed, and statutory dues listed with their due dates.
- **A short list of risks.** Overdue compliance, concentrated customers, cash gaps, weak controls: the things that could hurt you in the next six months.
- **An honest view of the books.** Which numbers can be trusted today, and which cannot yet.

## Days 31 to 60: build the rhythm

The second month is about cadence. The aim is a monthly close that finishes on a fixed date, every month, without heroics. That usually means a closing calendar, a short checklist for the accounts team, and a handful of reconciliations that happen weekly instead of at month end.

This is also when the first real management report appears. It should be short: a page or two that answers what happened, why, and what we will do about it. If it is twenty pages long, it is a data dump, not a report.

## Days 61 to 90: decide with the numbers

By the third month the numbers should start changing decisions. Typical examples:

- A thirteen-week cash forecast that lets you plan supplier payments and borrowing instead of reacting to them.
- Margin by product, customer or project, so pricing conversations rest on facts.
- A budget for the rest of the year that the team helped build and therefore believes.
- A banking or investor pack that can be produced in a day, not a fortnight.

## How to tell whether it is working

Three simple tests. First, do you get your monthly numbers on the same date every month? Second, has at least one significant decision in the last month been changed by something the numbers showed? Third, does your team know what is expected of them at month end without being chased?

If the answer to all three is yes by day 90, the engagement is working. If not, ask what is in the way. Sometimes it is data quality, sometimes people, sometimes the scope itself. A good virtual CFO will tell you plainly which it is.

## What a virtual CFO is not

A virtual CFO is not a replacement for your accountant or your auditor, and not a part-time bookkeeper. The role sits above the books: it turns them into decisions, keeps the business ready for lenders and investors, and builds the systems that let the team run the close without depending on any one person.

If you are weighing whether your business is ready for one, the honest test is this: are you making decisions today that you would make differently if you had better numbers? If the answer is yes, the conversation is worth having.
